The three ways AI receptionists are sold

1. DIY software (you set it up yourself)

This is the cheapest entry point. You subscribe to a platform, connect your phone number, write or upload your business information, and configure the call flows yourself. Typical pricing tiers look like this:

The catch is your time. A basic setup can take an afternoon; a good one — where the AI knows your services, your service area, your emergency policy, and how you price — takes days of testing and tuning. If your hourly time is valuable, the "cheap" option may not be cheap.

2. Done-for-you setup and management

A DFY provider builds the system around your business: your services, your scripts, your calendar rules, your escalation policy for true emergencies. Typical pricing is a one-time setup of $500 to $1,500 plus $497 to $997 per month for hosting, call costs, monitoring, and ongoing tuning.

You are paying for three things: the initial build, the ongoing adjustments as your business changes (seasonal services, new technicians, price changes), and someone to call when something sounds wrong. For businesses where one booked job is worth hundreds or thousands of dollars, this model usually pays for itself if it recovers even a handful of calls per month.

3. Established communication platforms

Platforms like Podium (typically around $399/month) and Birdeye (typically $299–$449/month) bundle AI-assisted messaging and answering with reviews, texting campaigns, and reputation management. If you want one platform for reviews, webchat, texting, and payments, these can make sense. If your only problem is "we miss calls and lose jobs," a dedicated AI receptionist is usually the more focused — and often cheaper — tool.

What actually drives the price

  • Call volume and minutes. Most plans include a block of minutes. A quiet office and a busy HVAC company in July are not the same product.
  • Voice vs. text only. Text-based chat and SMS automation is cheaper to run than natural voice conversation. Full voice answering costs more.
  • Booking and integrations. Connecting to your calendar is standard; deep integration with a CRM or field-service platform adds cost.
  • Hours of coverage. Business-hours-only answering is cheaper than true 24/7 coverage — and after-hours is exactly when high-value emergency calls arrive.
  • Languages and locations. Multilingual answering and multi-location routing raise the price on most platforms.
  • Human backup. Some services add a human overflow team for calls the AI can't handle. Useful, but it moves pricing toward a traditional answering service.

The hidden costs nobody puts on the pricing page

Overage minutes. Ask what a minute costs after your included block runs out, and whether the provider warns you or just bills you. Extra numbers and locations are often billed per line. Your own setup time on a DIY plan is a real cost — count it honestly. Finally, a badly configured AI is the most expensive option of all: if it quotes wrong prices or mishandles an emergency call, the "savings" vanish. Whatever you choose, pilot it on real calls and listen to the recordings before you fully commit.

What are missed calls costing you right now?

Run your own numbers before comparing quotes. Industry research consistently finds that about 62% of calls to small businesses go unanswered, and around 85% of callers who reach voicemail never call back — they call the next company on the list. Estimates put the average small business's annual loss from missed calls at $62,000 to $126,000.

Your math is simpler: take the calls you miss per week, multiply by your average job value, then by a realistic close rate. If you miss 10 calls a week, your average job is $400, and even a quarter of those callers would have booked, that's roughly $1,000 a week walking to competitors. Against that number, a $497/month system doesn't need to work miracles — it needs to save about one job a month.

Five questions to ask any provider before you sign

Whatever you buy — DIY software, a platform bundle, or a done-for-you service — these five questions will surface the real price faster than any pricing page:

  1. "What counts as a minute, and what happens when I run out?" Some plans round every call up, and overage rates vary enormously. A cheap plan with expensive overages is not a cheap plan in your busiest month — which, for trades like HVAC, is exactly the month the system matters most.
  2. "Can I hear it handle my calls before I commit?" Ask for a demo using your business name, your services, and your hardest scenarios: a pricing question, an emergency, a caller it can't help. If a provider won't do this, ask why.
  3. "Who fixes it when it says something wrong?" On DIY plans, the answer is you. On DFY plans, it should be the provider, quickly, and included in the monthly fee — confirm that tuning isn't billed as extra "professional services."
  4. "What does it integrate with, and what doesn't it?" Calendar booking is common; writing into your specific field-service software may not be. Get the integration list in writing and check your exact tools are on it.
  5. "How do I leave?" Month-to-month terms and a pilot period mean the provider has to keep earning the business. Long lock-in contracts on an unproven system shift all the risk to you.

A provider who answers all five clearly, in writing, is usually safe to pilot. A provider who dodges the first one is telling you where the real price is hidden.

How much does an AI receptionist cost per month?

DIY software typically costs $29 to $500 per month depending on call volume and features. A done-for-you service typically costs $500 to $1,500 in setup plus $497 to $997 per month, with setup and ongoing management included.

Is an AI receptionist cheaper than a human answering service?

Usually, yes for routine call handling. Human answering services typically bill per minute or per call, so costs rise with volume and after-hours coverage. AI plans are usually flat monthly fees, which makes them cheaper at moderate to high volumes — though human services still handle complex or sensitive calls better.

What hidden costs should I watch for?

Per-minute overage charges, additional phone numbers, integration fees, and your own configuration time. Always ask what happens when you exceed your plan's included minutes.
